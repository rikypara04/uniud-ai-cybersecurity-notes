Complexity and Information Theory

Course notes and materials

1 Course workspace

This section contains the processed lecture notes and source materials for Complexity and Information Theory.

1.1 Organisation

  • notes: processed lecture notes in Quarto format;
  • source-material/goodnotes: original handwritten Goodnotes exports;
  • source-material/teams: official material distributed through Teams;
  • source-material/external: additional relevant material;
  • references: academic references provided for the course.

2 Reusable prompt for generating a lecture note

Copy and use the following prompt whenever a new lecture note must be created or revised.

You are creating one English Quarto chapter for the course
"Complexity and Information Theory" in the MSc Artificial Intelligence
& Cybersecurity at the University of Udine.

MATERIAL I WILL PROVIDE

I will provide:

1. one Goodnotes PDF containing my handwritten notes for one specific
   lecture;
2. where relevant, the official course material distributed through
   Microsoft Teams;
3. academic books or papers provided as references for the course;
4. possibly the previous chapter, only when continuity with the
   preceding lecture must be checked.

SOURCE PRIORITY

Use the sources in this order:

1. The Goodnotes PDF determines the exact scope, order and boundaries
   of the lecture.
2. The official Teams material is used to verify terminology,
   notation, formulas, definitions, examples and missing details.
3. The provided books and academic references are used only to explain,
   verify or improve material that is already present in the lecture.
4. External online sources may be used only when the supplied sources
   are insufficient for verifying a specific point.

STRICT LECTURE BOUNDARY

The resulting chapter must contain exactly the topics taught in the
provided lecture.

Do not anticipate definitions, formulas, results, examples or
applications that belong to a later lecture.

Do not move a topic from another lecture into the current chapter merely
because it would make the exposition appear more complete.

If the books discuss a broader theory, use only the portions necessary
to explain the content actually appearing in the handwritten notes.

If the lecture only introduces a concept informally, preserve that
introductory level unless the handwritten notes or official material
develop it further.

ANALYSIS OF THE HANDWRITTEN NOTES

Before writing, inspect the entire Goodnotes PDF carefully and recover:

- the exact sequence of topics;
- all written sentences and short annotations;
- definitions and terminology;
- mathematical notation;
- formulas and derivations;
- worked examples and numerical calculations;
- arrows, diagrams and relationships;
- corrections and crossed-out material when their intended meaning is
  recoverable;
- highlighted or underlined concepts;
- remarks attributed to the lecturer;
- questions, doubts and incomplete passages.

Do not silently guess unreadable text. When a passage cannot be read
reliably, explicitly report the uncertainty before producing the final
chapter or mark the unresolved point clearly.

FIDELITY AND REWRITING

Write all substantive information contained in the lecture notes.

Do not produce a literal transcription when the handwritten wording is
fragmentary. Rewrite it as clear, connected academic English while
preserving:

- the original meaning;
- the original order;
- the notation used in the course;
- all examples and calculations;
- the level reached during that lecture.

Do not remove a detail merely because it appears elementary. If it was
written in the notes and contributes to the explanation, include it.

DEPTH OF EXPLANATION

The chapter must support real understanding and oral explanation, not
memorisation alone.

For every important definition, formula or result already present in
the lesson, explain as appropriate:

- what problem or question motivates it;
- what each symbol means;
- why the definition has that form;
- how the formula is derived or justified;
- which assumptions are required;
- how it should be interpreted intuitively;
- what changes in relevant limiting or special cases;
- how the example in the notes illustrates the concept;
- which common confusion is directly relevant.

For example, if the lecture introduces

    I(m) = -log_2 p(m),

do not merely state the formula. If supported by the lecture and its
references, explain why information should decrease with probability,
why independent information should add, why this leads to a logarithm,
why the minus sign is needed and why base 2 gives bits.

However, do not add later concepts such as entropy, mutual information
or coding theorems unless they are actually introduced in the same
lecture.

USE OF BOOKS AND REFERENCES

References must be used actively but conservatively.

A reference may be used to:

- clarify a sentence from the handwritten notes;
- make an implicit mathematical step explicit;
- justify a formula already introduced;
- improve an explanation;
- verify notation or terminology;
- complete an example already begun in the lecture;
- correct a clear transcription error.

A reference must not be used to:

- add a new topic unrelated to the lecture;
- reproduce an entire textbook section;
- introduce later course content;
- replace the lecturer's structure with the book's structure;
- make the chapter unnecessarily long.

STRUCTURE OF THE QMD CHAPTER

Follow the actual sequence of the lecture. Do not impose a rigid
textbook template.

Use:

- one clear H1 title supplied through YAML;
- H1 sections for the major phases of the lecture;
- H2 and H3 subsections only where the lecture genuinely changes topic;
- short paragraphs;
- bullet lists for properties, alternatives or comparisons;
- numbered lists for procedures or derivations;
- display equations for important formulas;
- tables when they materially improve comparison, classification,
  notation, execution traces or the visibility of a derivation;
- diagrams when they materially improve understanding of a process,
  machine configuration, control flow, transformation, simulation,
  hierarchy or relationship;
- callouts only for especially important warnings, interpretations or
  distinctions;
- visually distinct red boxes for theorems, propositions, lemmas,
  invariance results and central formal statements.

Do not automatically add generic sections such as "Learning
objectives", "Prerequisites", "Applications", "Common mistakes",
"Self-test" or "Summary". Add one only if it improves this specific
lecture and is supported by its contents.

VISUAL EXPLANATIONS: DIAGRAMS, TABLES AND FORMAL BOXES

The chapter must not consist only of continuous prose and equations when
the material contains structures or relationships that can be made
clearer visually.

For every major section, actively decide whether a diagram, table or
formal box would materially improve understanding. Do not add visuals
decoratively or merely to increase their number.

Use Mermaid diagrams for conceptual and procedural structures such as:

- control flow and machine execution;
- encodings and transformations;
- simulations between computational models;
- dependencies among definitions or results;
- alternative formulations of the same problem;
- growth processes and sequences of operations.

Every Mermaid diagram must:

- use a standard Quarto `mermaid` code block;
- include a concise `fig-cap` stating what the reader should learn;
- use short, readable labels rather than paragraphs inside nodes;
- use a consistent colour scheme across the chapter;
- distinguish semantically different node types through colour only
  when that distinction is meaningful;
- reproduce only relationships supported by the lecture;
- remain readable in both light and dark themes.

Use Markdown tables for compact comparisons such as:

- two or more cost criteria or computational models;
- symbols, definitions and roles;
- instruction names, effects and purposes;
- problem variants and their required outputs;
- step-by-step values in a worked example;
- assumptions and their consequences.

Do not use a table when its cells would contain long paragraphs or when
a short list would be clearer. Never place executable code blocks inside
tables.

Use a custom red formal box for every theorem, proposition, lemma,
invariance result or central formal statement that the lecture treats
as a result. Use this reusable chapter-local style immediately after the
YAML front matter:

    ```{=html}
    <style>
    .theorem-box {
      border: 2px solid #b02a37;
      border-left: 0.55rem solid #b02a37;
      border-radius: 0.45rem;
      padding: 1rem 1.15rem 0.85rem;
      margin: 1.25rem 0;
      background: rgba(176, 42, 55, 0.07);
    }
    .theorem-box > :first-child { margin-top: 0; }
    .theorem-box > :last-child { margin-bottom: 0; }
    .theorem-title {
      color: #b02a37;
      font-weight: 700;
      margin-bottom: 0.45rem;
    }
    @media (prefers-color-scheme: dark) {
      .theorem-box {
        background: rgba(255, 107, 122, 0.10);
        border-color: #ff6b7a;
      }
      .theorem-title { color: #ff8a96; }
    }
    </style>
    ```

Use the box in this form:

    ::: {.theorem-box}
    <div class="theorem-title">Theorem or result name</div>

    Formal statement, with equations if needed.
    :::

Do not label a statement as a theorem if the lecture presents it only
as an intuition, convention, thesis or warning. In that case, use an
accurate title such as "Extended Church--Turing Thesis",
"Key equivalence" or "Interpretation".

When the handwritten notes contain a diagram, reconstruct its
information faithfully in Mermaid or, when Mermaid is unsuitable, in
small accessible HTML/CSS. Preserve the logical content rather than
imitating the handwriting literally. Add an accessible label or caption.

MATHEMATICAL RENDERING RULES

- Use only MathJax-compatible LaTeX inside $...$ and $$...$$.
- Prefer robust commands such as `\mathcal`, `\mathrm`,
  `\operatorname`, `\text`, `\langle`, `\rangle`, `\mid` and
  `\Longleftrightarrow`.
- Do not use `\textsc` in mathematics because it may be unsupported or
  rendered inconsistently in Quarto HTML.
- Write named languages with stable notation, for example
  `\mathcal{L}_{3\text{-COL}}`, and define that notation explicitly.
- Keep textual material inside equations short. If a condition is long,
  use `\left\{ ... \middle| ... \right\}` and `\text{...}` carefully.
- Check every displayed formula for balanced braces, supported commands
  and correct rendering before delivery.

FRONT MATTER

Use this minimal front matter:

    ---
    title: "Descriptive title based on the lecture"
    subtitle: "Lecture N - D Month YYYY"
    format:
      html:
        toc: true
        toc-depth: 3
        number-sections: true
    ---

Do not add author or date metadata because they create unnecessary
Author and Published blocks on the website.

FORMATTING RULES

- Write the chapter in English.
- Use standard Quarto Markdown.
- Use LaTeX notation inside $...$ and $$...$$.
- Define every symbol before or immediately after first use.
- Keep notation consistent with the lecturer's notation.
- Do not use unsupported HTML for mathematical content.
- Do not add fake citations, titles, page numbers or bibliographic data.
- Preserve exact numerical values and calculations from the lecture.
- Do not copy editor-generated HTML classes such as `___1qwroh2`;
  use ordinary Markdown headings and clean chapter-local classes only.

SOURCES SECTION

End the chapter with a short "Sources" section containing only the
sources actually used.

Distinguish:

- the Goodnotes lecture PDF;
- the relevant official Teams document;
- the exact books or papers consulted.

Do not list a book if it was not actually used for that chapter.

QUALITY CHECK BEFORE DELIVERY

Before delivering the QMD file, verify that:

1. every legible substantive item in the Goodnotes PDF is represented;
2. the order follows the lecture;
3. no later lecture content has been anticipated;
4. formulas and examples have been checked;
5. important "why" questions are explained where supported;
6. reference material is pertinent and not excessive;
7. the chapter is suitable for studying and for oral explanation;
8. the front matter contains no author or published-date metadata;
9. the output is one complete, ready-to-save `.qmd` file;
10. every LaTeX command is supported by Quarto's HTML mathematics
    renderer;
11. diagrams, tables or formal boxes are included wherever they
    materially improve understanding;
12. every visual is accurate, captioned where appropriate, readable in
    light and dark mode, and supported by the lecture;
13. theorem and central-result boxes use the consistent red style;
14. no visual anticipates material from a later lecture.

DELIVERABLE

Return one complete `.qmd` file ready to be saved in:

    01-complexity-information-theory/notes/

Use a two-digit chronological filename such as:

    03-descriptive-topic.qmd
