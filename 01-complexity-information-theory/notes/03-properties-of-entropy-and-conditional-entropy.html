Properties of Entropy and Conditional Entropy

Lecture 3 - 8 October 2026

1 Basic properties of entropy

Let \(X\) be a discrete random variable with finite alphabet \(\mathcal{X}\) and probability mass function \(p\). Its entropy is

\[ H(X)=H(p)=-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(x)\log_2p(x). \]

The lecture studies two fundamental properties of this quantity.

1.1 Non-negativity

Entropy is always non-negative:

\[ H(p)\geq0. \]

For every value \(x\) with \(p(x)>0\), we have \(p(x)\leq1\), hence

\[ \log_2p(x)\leq0. \]

Therefore,

\[ -p(x)\log_2p(x)\geq0, \]

and the sum of these terms cannot be negative.

Moreover,

\[ H(p)=0 \]

if and only if there exists a value \(x_0\in\mathcal{X}\) such that

\[ p(x_0)=1. \]

Indeed, a term \(-p(x)\log_2p(x)\) is zero only when \(p(x)=0\) or \(p(x)=1\). For the whole sum to be zero, one value must therefore have probability \(1\) and all the others probability \(0\).

This matches the interpretation of entropy as uncertainty: if \(X\) is known in advance with certainty, observing it provides no new information.

1.2 Maximum entropy on a finite alphabet

If the alphabet has size

\[ |\mathcal{X}|=k, \]

then

\[ H(p)\leq\log_2k. \]

Equality holds if and only if \(X\) is uniformly distributed:

\[ p(x)=\frac1k \qquad \text{for every }x\in\mathcal{X}. \]

For the uniform distribution \(u=(1/k,\ldots,1/k)\),

\[ \begin{aligned} H(u) &=-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac1k\log_2\frac1k\\ &=-k\frac1k\log_2\frac1k\\ &=\log_2k. \end{aligned} \]

The uniform distribution is the most uncertain distribution on a fixed alphabet because it gives no preference to any possible value. Any imbalance makes some outcomes more predictable and decreases entropy.

2 Why the uniform distribution maximises entropy

The proof in the lecture uses Jensen’s inequality.

2.1 Concave functions

A function \(f\) is concave when, for \(a,b\) in its domain and \(\lambda\in[0,1]\),

\[ f\bigl(\lambda a+(1-\lambda)b\bigr) \geq \lambda f(a)+(1-\lambda)f(b). \]

Geometrically, the graph of a concave function lies above the chord joining any two points of the graph.

For a twice differentiable function, the condition

\[ f''(x)\leq0 \]

implies concavity. The logarithm is strictly concave because

\[ \frac{d^2}{dx^2}\log_2x =-\frac{1}{x^2\ln2}<0 \qquad (x>0). \]

2.2 Jensen’s inequality

For a concave function \(f\), values \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\), and non-negative weights \(\lambda_i\) satisfying \(\sum_i\lambda_i=1\), Jensen’s inequality states that

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i f(x_i) \leq f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i x_i\right). \]

In words:

The weighted average of the function values is no greater than the function evaluated at the weighted average.

The direction is reversed for convex functions.

2.3 Application to entropy

Let the positive probabilities of \(p\) be \(p_1,\ldots,p_m\), where \(m\leq k\). Write entropy as

\[ H(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_i\log_2\frac1{p_i}. \]

Apply Jensen’s inequality to the concave function \(\log_2\), using weights \(p_i\) and values \(1/p_i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} H(p) &=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_i\log_2\frac1{p_i}\\ &\leq \log_2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_i\frac1{p_i}\right)\\ &=\log_2m\\ &\leq\log_2k. \end{aligned} \]

Because the logarithm is strictly concave, equality in Jensen’s inequality requires all values \(1/p_i\) to be equal. Thus all positive probabilities must be equal. Equality with \(\log_2k\) additionally requires \(m=k\), so

\[ p_i=\frac1k \]

for every value in the alphabet.

This proves both the upper bound and its equality condition.

3 Entropy of the animal distribution

The running example uses the species random variable

\[ X\in\{c,d,f,w\}, \]

with probability mass function

\[ p_X=\left(\frac12,\frac14,\frac18,\frac18\right). \]

The surprisals of cat, dog, fox and wolf are respectively

\[ 1,\;2,\;3,\;3\text{ bits}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} H(X) &=\frac12\cdot1 +\frac14\cdot2 +\frac18\cdot3 +\frac18\cdot3\\ &=\frac74\\ &=1.75\text{ bits}. \end{aligned} \]

Since \(X\) has four possible values,

\[ H(X)\leq\log_2 4=2\text{ bits}. \]

The inequality is strict because the species are not uniformly distributed:

\[ 1.75<2. \]

For comparison, if all four species had probability \(1/4\), the entropy would be exactly \(2\) bits.

4 Joint entropy

The lecture then considers two discrete random variables:

  • \(X\), the species of the animal, with alphabet \(\mathcal{X}=\{c,d,f,w\}\);
  • \(Y\), the sensor output, with alphabet \(\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}\), where \(Y=1\) means alert and \(Y=0\) means no alert.

Before defining joint entropy, it is important to distinguish three probability mass functions.

4.1 Joint and marginal probability mass functions

The joint probability mass function is

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y). \]

For a particular pair \((x,y)\), this is the probability that both events occur simultaneously: the species is \(x\) and the sensor output is \(y\).

The marginal probability mass function of \(X\) is

\[ p_X(x)=P(X=x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p_{X,Y}(x,y). \]

The variable \(Y\) is summed out because only the probability of \(X=x\) is required. Similarly, the marginal pmf of \(Y\) is

\[ p_Y(y)=P(Y=y)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p_{X,Y}(x,y). \]

Thus:

  • \(p_{X,Y}(x,y)\) refers to one cell of the joint table;
  • \(p_X(x)\) refers to the total probability of column \(x\);
  • \(p_Y(y)\) refers to the total probability of row \(y\).

For the running example, the joint distribution is

\(p_{X,Y}(x,y)\) \(X=c\) \(X=d\) \(X=f\) \(X=w\) \(p_Y(y)\)
\(Y=0\) \(3/8\) \(1/8\) \(0\) \(0\) \(1/2\)
\(Y=1\) \(1/8\) \(1/8\) \(1/8\) \(1/8\) \(1/2\)
\(p_X(x)\) \(1/2\) \(1/4\) \(1/8\) \(1/8\) \(1\)

For example,

\[ p_{X,Y}(c,0)=P(X=c,Y=0)=\frac38. \]

The cat marginal is obtained by adding the two cells in the cat column:

\[ p_X(c) =p_{X,Y}(c,0)+p_{X,Y}(c,1) =\frac38+\frac18 =\frac12. \]

The no-alert marginal is obtained by adding the first row:

\[ p_Y(0) =\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p_{X,Y}(x,0) =\frac38+\frac18+0+0 =\frac12. \]

Therefore, \(p_X\) and \(p_Y\) are not new unrelated distributions. They are derived from the joint distribution by summing over the variable that is not of interest.

4.2 Deriving the joint-entropy formula

For one outcome \(x\) of \(X\), the self-information is

\[ I_X(x)=-\log_2p_X(x). \]

The pair \((X,Y)\) is itself a discrete random variable whose possible outcomes are all pairs

\[ (x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}. \]

The probability of the pair \((x,y)\) is

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y). \]

Therefore, the self-information obtained when the observed pair is \((x,y)\) is

\[ I_{X,Y}(x,y)=-\log_2p_{X,Y}(x,y). \]

Entropy is the expected self-information. Taking the expectation over every possible pair gives

\[ \begin{aligned} H(X,Y) &=\mathbb{E}[I_{X,Y}(X,Y)]\\ &=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}} p_{X,Y}(x,y)I_{X,Y}(x,y)\\ &=-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}} p_{X,Y}(x,y)\log_2p_{X,Y}(x,y). \end{aligned} \]

The compact notation

\[ H(X,Y)=-\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_2p(x,y) \]

means exactly the same thing. In that abbreviated expression, \(p(x,y)\) stands for the joint pmf \(p_{X,Y}(x,y)\), and the sum runs over all pairs \((x,y)\).

The formula is therefore not a separate rule to memorise. It is the ordinary entropy formula applied to the single random object \((X,Y)\).

For the running example, the table contains one non-zero cell of probability \(3/8\) and five cells of probability \(1/8\). Cells with probability zero contribute zero by the convention \(0\log 0=0\). Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} H(X,Y) &=-\frac38\log_2\frac38 -5\cdot\frac18\log_2\frac18\\ &=\frac38\log_2\frac83+\frac{15}{8}\\ &\approx2.406\text{ bits}. \end{aligned} \]

Joint entropy measures the average information needed to identify both the species and the sensor output.

5 Conditional entropy

Suppose that the species value \(X=x\) is known. The relevant uncertainty is no longer described by the marginal pmf \(p_Y(y)\), but by the conditional pmf

\[ p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x) =P(Y=y\mid X=x) =\frac{p_{X,Y}(x,y)}{p_X(x)}, \]

provided that \(p_X(x)>0\).

For this fixed value \(x\), the entropy of the conditional distribution is

\[ H(Y\mid X=x) =-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}} p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x) \log_2p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x). \]

This number answers a specific question:

After learning that \(X=x\), how uncertain is the value of \(Y\)?

Different values of \(x\) may leave different amounts of uncertainty about \(Y\). For example, knowing that the animal is a fox may determine the alert completely, while knowing that the animal is a cat may still leave two possible alert values.

5.1 Deriving the average conditional-entropy formula

Before observing \(X\), the value \(x\) that will occur is not known. Each possible value \(x\) occurs with probability \(p_X(x)\). Therefore, the overall remaining uncertainty about \(Y\) after \(X\) is revealed is the weighted average of the separate conditional entropies:

\[ H(Y\mid X) =\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}} p_X(x)H(Y\mid X=x). \]

The weight \(p_X(x)\) is necessary because some values of \(X\) occur more frequently than others. Without these weights, rare and common cases would incorrectly contribute equally to the average.

Substituting the definition of \(H(Y\mid X=x)\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} H(Y\mid X) &=\sum_x p_X(x) \left[-\sum_y p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x) \log_2p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\right]\\ &=-\sum_x\sum_y p_X(x)p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x) \log_2p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x). \end{aligned} \]

Using the product rule

\[ p_X(x)p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=p_{X,Y}(x,y), \]

we obtain the equivalent formula

\[ H(Y\mid X) =-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}} p_{X,Y}(x,y) \log_2p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x). \]

In compact notation,

\[ H(Y\mid X) =-\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_2p(y\mid x). \]

The two formulas express the same idea:

  • \(\sum_x p_X(x)H(Y\mid X=x)\) emphasises the weighted average over the possible known values of \(X\);
  • \(-\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_2p(y\mid x)\) expands that average over all joint outcomes.

5.2 Uncertainty of the alert given the species

The conditional alert probabilities can be derived directly from the joint probability table shown above.

For each species (x), the probability of receiving an alert after learning that the observed species is (x) is

\[ P(Y=1\mid X=x) = \frac{P(X=x,Y=1)}{P(X=x)} = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x,1)}{p_X(x)}. \]

The numerator is the joint probability found in the (Y=1) row and in the column corresponding to species (x). The denominator is the marginal probability of that species, shown at the bottom of the same column.

For a cat,

\[ P(Y=1\mid X=c) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(c,1)}{p_X(c)} = \frac{1/8}{1/2} = \frac14. \]

For a dog,

\[ P(Y=1\mid X=d) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(d,1)}{p_X(d)} = \frac{1/8}{1/4} = \frac12. \]

For a fox,

\[ P(Y=1\mid X=f) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(f,1)}{p_X(f)} = \frac{1/8}{1/8} = 1. \]

Similarly, for a wolf,

\[ P(Y=1\mid X=w) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(w,1)}{p_X(w)} = \frac{1/8}{1/8} = 1. \]

Therefore, the complete conditional distributions of (Y), written in the order ((Y=0,Y=1)), are

\[ p_{Y\mid X}(\cdot\mid c) = \left(\frac34,\frac14\right), \]

\[ p_{Y\mid X}(\cdot\mid d) = \left(\frac12,\frac12\right), \]

and

\[ p_{Y\mid X}(\cdot\mid f) = p_{Y\mid X}(\cdot\mid w) = (0,1). \]

Using the binary entropy function

\[ h(t) = -t\log_2t-(1-t)\log_2(1-t), \]

we obtain

\[ H(Y\mid X=c) = h\left(\frac14\right) \approx 0.811, \]

\[ H(Y\mid X=d) = h\left(\frac12\right) = 1, \]

and

\[ H(Y\mid X=f) = H(Y\mid X=w) = h(1) = 0. \]

The conditional entropy (H(YX)) is the weighted average of these four values. Each conditional entropy must be weighted by the marginal probability of the corresponding species:

\[ \begin{aligned} H(Y\mid X) &= p_X(c)H(Y\mid X=c) +p_X(d)H(Y\mid X=d)\\ &\quad +p_X(f)H(Y\mid X=f) +p_X(w)H(Y\mid X=w)\\ &= \frac12h\left(\frac14\right) +\frac14h\left(\frac12\right) +\frac18\cdot0 +\frac18\cdot0\\ &\approx0.656\text{ bits}. \end{aligned} \]

Knowing that the animal is a fox or a wolf completely determines the sensor output, because the sensor always produces an alert in those cases. For cats and dogs, both alert values remain possible, so the conditional entropy is positive. The conditional alert probabilities are

\[ P(Y=1\mid X=c)=\frac14, \qquad P(Y=1\mid X=d)=\frac12, \]

and

\[ P(Y=1\mid X=f)=P(Y=1\mid X=w)=1. \]

Using the binary entropy function

\[ h(t)=-t\log_2t-(1-t)\log_2(1-t), \]

we obtain

\[ H(Y\mid X=c)=h\left(\frac14\right)\approx0.811, \]

\[ H(Y\mid X=d)=h\left(\frac12\right)=1, \]

and

\[ H(Y\mid X=f)=H(Y\mid X=w)=h(1)=0. \]

The average conditional entropy is therefore

\[ \begin{aligned} H(Y\mid X) &=p_X(c)H(Y\mid X=c) +p_X(d)H(Y\mid X=d)\\ &\quad +p_X(f)H(Y\mid X=f) +p_X(w)H(Y\mid X=w)\\ &=\frac12h\left(\frac14\right) +\frac14h\left(\frac12\right) +\frac18\cdot0 +\frac18\cdot0\\ &\approx0.656\text{ bits}. \end{aligned} \]

Knowing the species does not always determine the alert. The alert remains uncertain for cats and dogs, but it is certain for foxes and wolves.

5.3 Uncertainty of the species given the alert

The conditional distributions of \(X\) are

\[ p_{X\mid Y}(\cdot\mid0) =\left(\frac34,\frac14,0,0\right) \]

and

\[ p_{X\mid Y}(\cdot\mid1) =\left(\frac14,\frac14,\frac14,\frac14\right). \]

Here \(p_{X\mid Y}(\cdot\mid0)\) means the entire conditional distribution of \(X\) after observing \(Y=0\); the dot is a placeholder for all possible values of \(X\).

Thus

\[ H(X\mid Y=0)=h\left(\frac14\right)\approx0.811\text{ bits}, \]

while

\[ H(X\mid Y=1)=\log_2 4=2\text{ bits}. \]

Since

\[ p_Y(0)=p_Y(1)=\frac12, \]

the average is

\[ \begin{aligned} H(X\mid Y) &=\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}}p_Y(y)H(X\mid Y=y)\\ &=\frac12H(X\mid Y=0)+\frac12H(X\mid Y=1)\\ &=\frac12\cdot0.811+\frac12\cdot2\\ &\approx1.406\text{ bits}. \end{aligned} \]

The two conditional entropies are not equal:

\[ H(Y\mid X)\approx0.656 \neq H(X\mid Y)\approx1.406. \]

They answer different questions. \(H(Y\mid X)\) asks how uncertain the alert remains after the species is known. \(H(X\mid Y)\) asks how uncertain the species remains after the alert is known.

6 Chain rule for entropy

The joint and conditional entropies satisfy

\[ H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y\mid X). \]

To understand why, use the product rule

\[ p(x,y)=p(x)p(y\mid x). \]

Taking logarithms turns this product into a sum:

\[ \log_2p(x,y) =\log_2p(x)+\log_2p(y\mid x). \]

Multiplying by \(-p(x,y)\) and summing over \(x\) and \(y\) gives

\[ H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y\mid X). \]

The chain rule also holds in the other order:

\[ H(X,Y)=H(Y)+H(X\mid Y). \]

For the running example,

\[ H(X)+H(Y\mid X) =1.75+0.656 =2.406, \]

and

\[ H(Y)+H(X\mid Y) =1+1.406 =2.406. \]

Both decompositions equal the directly computed joint entropy.

Operationally, describing the pair \((X,Y)\) requires the same average information as describing \(X\) first and then describing \(Y\) with \(X\) already known.

7 Reduction of uncertainty and mutual information

From the chain rule,

\[ H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y\mid X)=H(Y)+H(X\mid Y). \]

Rearranging gives

\[ H(X)-H(X\mid Y) =H(Y)-H(Y\mid X). \]

In the running example,

\[ H(X)-H(X\mid Y) =1.75-1.406 =0.344\text{ bits}, \]

and

\[ H(Y)-H(Y\mid X) =1-0.656 =0.344\text{ bits}. \]

The same reduction appears in both directions. This symmetric quantity is called the mutual information:

\[ I(X;Y) =H(X)-H(X\mid Y) =H(Y)-H(Y\mid X). \]

It measures how much knowing one variable reduces, on average, the uncertainty about the other. Equivalently, it measures how dependent \(X\) and \(Y\) are: if knowing one variable does not change uncertainty about the other, the mutual information is zero.

The lecture introduces this interpretation without yet developing the broader theory of mutual information.

8 A particular observation can increase uncertainty

The average conditional entropy satisfies

\[ H(X\mid Y)\leq H(X), \]

but this does not mean that every individual observation \(Y=y\) decreases entropy.

In the example,

\[ H(X)=1.75\text{ bits}, \]

whereas after observing an alert,

\[ H(X\mid Y=1)=2\text{ bits}. \]

The alert makes all four species equally likely, so uncertainty increases for this particular observation.

There is no contradiction because the inequality concerns the average over all observations:

\[ H(X\mid Y) =\sum_y p_Y(y)H(X\mid Y=y) \approx1.406<1.75. \]

The no-alert observation reduces uncertainty enough to compensate for the increase caused by the alert observation.
