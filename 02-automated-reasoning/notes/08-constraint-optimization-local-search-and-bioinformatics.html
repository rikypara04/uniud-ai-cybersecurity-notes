Constraint Optimization, Large Neighbourhood Search, and Bioinformatics Models

Lecture 8 - 23 October 2026

1 Constraint Optimization Problems

A Constraint Optimization Problem (COP) extends a Constraint Satisfaction Problem with an objective function. It is a tuple

\[ \langle V,D,C,f\rangle, \]

where:

  • \(V=\{V_1,\ldots,V_n\}\) is the set of variables;
  • \(D=\{D_1,\ldots,D_n\}\) contains their domains;
  • \(C\) is the set of constraints;
  • \(f:D_1\times\cdots\times D_n\to A\) maps complete assignments to an ordered set \(A\).

A constraint on variables \(V_{i_1},\ldots,V_{i_m}\) is a relation

\[ c\subseteq D_{i_1}\times\cdots\times D_{i_m}. \]

An assignment \(\omega\) is admissible when \(\omega(V_i)\in D_i\) for every variable and every constraint contains the tuple of assigned values. A COP asks for an admissible assignment minimising \(f\), or equivalently maximising an objective after an appropriate sign change.

2 Solving COPs with Constraint Programming

Constraint Programming explores a propagation-labeling tree. For satisfaction, finding one admissible leaf may be enough. For complete optimisation, however, the solver must also establish that no unexplored branch contains a better solution.

When a feasible solution with value \(b\) is found in a minimisation problem, \(b\) becomes the incumbent. The solver can add the improvement requirement

\[ f<b. \]

Propagation may then remove branches whose best possible value cannot beat \(b\). Each improved solution tightens the bound again.

The lecture emphasises four practical consequences:

  • complete optimisation may require exploring the whole remaining tree;
  • objective bounds learned from incumbents can substantially prune it;
  • search may restart after an improved bound, possibly with another strategy;
  • when a good solution is sufficient, a timeout can return the best incumbent found without proving optimality.

3 Branch and bound on knapsack

The worked example uses a knapsack of capacity \(50\) and three goods:

Good Weight Profit
grappa \(17\) \(10\)
whisky \(10\) \(6\)
chocolate \(4\) \(2\)

Let \(G\), \(W\), and \(C\) be the selected quantities. The model shown on the slide uses positive domains and the capacity constraint

\[ 17G+10W+4C\le50, \]

with objective

\[ f(G,W,C)=10G+6W+2C. \]

Propagation first tightens the quantity domains using the capacity. Search then branches on quantities. Once the assignment

\[ G=1,\qquad W=2,\qquad C=3 \]

is found, its value is

\[ 10+12+6=28. \]

The incumbent becomes \(28\). A node whose objective upper bound is only \(26\) cannot improve the incumbent and fails immediately. The slide shows the same value \(28\) reached in another branch, illustrating that a second feasible solution may tie the incumbent without improving it.

The symbol \(k\) in the tree denotes an objective bound available at a node. The key distinction is between the value of a complete solution and an optimistic bound for a partial assignment.

4 Why complete search may be impractical

A huge propagation-labeling tree can require more time than is available. Exact branch and bound moves through the interior of the tree and seeks a proof of optimality. Local search starts from a complete candidate and moves along the frontier of complete assignments.

The deck lists several local-search metaheuristics:

  • Monte Carlo methods;
  • simulated annealing;
  • particle swarm methods;
  • ant-colony methods;
  • genetic methods.

They use randomised choices to seek improvement. Unlike complete search, they generally do not prove that the best visited solution is globally optimal.

5 Neighbourhoods and moves

Given a feasible assignment \(\omega\), a neighbourhood function \(N\) returns a set \(N(\omega)\) of feasible assignments considered easily reachable from \(\omega\). A move is the operation used to generate neighbours, such as swapping two variable values.

A local-search design must specify:

  1. how to construct an initial solution;
  2. the neighbourhood or allowed moves;
  3. how to choose a move;
  4. how much of the neighbourhood to inspect;
  5. how to avoid loops or other undesirable behaviour;
  6. when to stop, often after a fixed number of non-improving steps.

For minimisation, hill climbing chooses

\[ \omega'\in N(\omega) \]

such that

\[ f(\omega')=\min\{f(\mu):\mu\in N(\omega)\}. \]

A local minimum is a solution that is no worse than every neighbour, but it need not be globally optimal.

6 Four queens as a local-search example

The lecture uses four queens and defines cost as the number of attacks. A move swaps two selected positions. Some swaps reduce the cost strongly, whereas another choice can return to a configuration with 12 attacks.

The sequence of illustrated costs includes

\[ 12\to4\to0 \]

for a favourable route, but also a route from cost \(4\) back to cost \(12\). The lesson is not that every move improves the solution. The algorithm must decide whether to accept only improving moves, permit temporary worsening, or enlarge the neighbourhood.

A small neighbourhood based on one swap may miss a better configuration that requires coordinated changes. The next idea is therefore to free several variables and let Constraint Programming optimise the resulting subproblem.

7 Large Neighbourhood Search

Large Neighbourhood Search (LNS) is presented as a hybrid of local search and Constraint Programming. It was introduced by Shaw in 1998 and used in the Comet language.

Given a current solution \(s\) of COP \(\langle X,D,C,f\rangle\):

  1. choose a subset \(N\subseteq X\) of variables, often randomly or by a destruction heuristic;
  2. keep the values of \(X\setminus N\) fixed as in \(s\);
  3. unassign the variables in \(N\);
  4. use a CP solver to optimise the induced subproblem;
  5. accept an improving solution if one is found;
  6. otherwise choose another neighbourhood;
  7. stop after \(k\) consecutive non-improving rounds or after a global timeout.

The neighbourhood includes the incumbent itself because restoring the removed values is always an admissible repair. A larger destruction rate produces a larger neighbourhood: it offers more opportunities for improvement but makes each CP subproblem harder.

7.1 Old MiniZinc support

The slides document an old MiniZinc LNS interface:

int: iterations = 100;
float: destructionRate = 0.3;
int: time_ms = 5 * 1000;

solve search
  lns_min(obj, x, iterations, destructionRate, time_ms);

Here x is the decision-variable list and obj stores the objective. The lecture explicitly states that this library is no longer maintained. The syntax is historical course material, not a recommendation that the interface remains available.

The slides also mention solver-independent LNS work, a constraint-based local-search backend, CLP implementations, and an ad-hoc GPU solver used for protein-structure prediction.

7.2 Warm starts and a hand-made LNS

MiniZinc can compose searches using seq_search and can use restart schedules. The manual’s warm-start annotation is presented as a component for constructing LNS manually:

warm_start(<vars>, <vals>)

<vars> is a one-dimensional array of integer variables and <vals> is an equally long array of suggested starting values. The values may describe a partial or even invalid starting point; support depends on the solver.

A restart improves robustness by leaving an unproductive region. A warm start provides information about the incumbent around which the new search should begin. These mechanisms are related but not identical: restarting controls when search starts again, while a warm start suggests values for the new run.

8 Bioinformatics applications

The second part of the lecture presents two applications from computational biology:

  1. haplotype inference;
  2. protein-structure prediction.

The purpose is to show how discrete modelling and optimisation arise outside traditional scheduling and puzzle examples.

9 Haplotype inference

Chromosomes are DNA regions organised in pairs in diploid organisms. One chromosome of each pair is inherited from each parent. A haplotype records the sequence inherited from one parent, while a genotype combines the corresponding pair.

At selected genomic positions, Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) are observed. The slides focus on common substitutions such as \(C\leftrightarrow T\) and \(A\leftrightarrow G\).

9.1 Binary SNP encoding

At each biallelic SNP site, one allele is encoded as \(0\) and the other as \(1\). Thus a haplotype is a word over \(\{0,1\}\).

A genotype position records:

  • 0 when both haplotypes contain 0;
  • 1 when both contain 1;
  • 2 when the two haplotypes disagree.

Define the combination operator \(\oplus\) by

\[ 0\oplus0=0, \qquad 1\oplus1=1, \qquad 0\oplus1=1\oplus0=2. \]

Applied positionwise, two haplotypes generate one genotype. The lecture example is

\[ 0010\oplus0101=0222. \]

The inverse is not unique. The same genotype 0222 can also be explained by 0110 and 0001. Every 2 permits two orientations, so a genotype with many heterogeneous positions can admit exponentially many haplotype pairs.

9.2 Formal explanation relation

Let

\[ H\subseteq\{0,1\}^n, \qquad G\subseteq\{0,1,2\}^n. \]

A pair \(\{h_1,h_2\}\) explains genotype \(g\) exactly when, for each position \(i\):

\[ g_i\ne2 \implies h_{1,i}=h_{2,i}=g_i, \]

and

\[ g_i=2 \implies h_{1,i}\ne h_{2,i}. \]

A haplotype set \(H\) explains genotype set \(G\) when every \(g\in G\) has at least one explaining pair in \(H\).

The pure-parsimony problem seeks the smallest explanatory set. The decision form asks whether an explanatory set of size \(k\) exists. The slide states that this decision problem is NP-complete.

Biological experiments make genotypes easier and cheaper to obtain than the two underlying haplotypes. Computation is therefore used to infer a parsimonious explanation of population data.

10 From DNA to proteins

The lecture next recalls the central dogma:

\[ \text{DNA}\longrightarrow\text{mRNA}\longrightarrow\text{protein}, \]

through transcription and translation. Translation reads mRNA three bases at a time. The universal genetic code maps each triplet, or codon, to an amino acid or stop signal. Several codons may encode the same amino acid.

Proteins are linear chains of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. Twenty common amino-acid types are represented by letters

\[ \{A,\ldots,Z\}\setminus\{B,J,O,U,X,Z\}. \]

The backbone has two relevant rotational degrees of freedom per amino acid in the explanation given by the slides. Therefore, a chain of \(n\) amino acids has approximately \(2n\) degrees of freedom. Typical proteins in the lecture range from about 50 to 500 amino acids, leading to a very large conformational search space.

11 Protein-structure prediction

The input is the primary structure, the amino-acid sequence. The output is the position of each amino acid in space, producing a candidate tertiary structure.

Proteins fold spontaneously in their natural environment, do so consistently, and typically fold quickly. The resulting shape is influenced by non-covalent interactions, including electrostatic effects, excluded volume, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals interactions, and bridges, together with the rigidity of the backbone and interactions with water, ions, and ligands.

A cost function approximating these physical effects can score candidate conformations. Searching for the globally optimal score is computationally difficult, even in strongly simplified models.

12 The HP lattice model

The lecture makes two simplifications:

  1. represent each amino acid by one point and classify it only as hydrophobic (h) or polar (p);
  2. place amino acids on a two-dimensional square lattice.

The input is

\[ S=(s_1,\ldots,s_n), \qquad s_i\in\{h,p\}. \]

A folding is a function

\[ \varphi:\{1,\ldots,n\}\to\mathbb N^2. \]

12.1 Chain-adjacency constraint

Consecutive amino acids remain adjacent on the lattice:

\[ \operatorname{next}(\varphi(i),\varphi(i+1)) \]

for every \(i<n\).

For coordinates \((X_1,Y_1)\) and \((X_2,Y_2)\),

\[ \operatorname{next}((X_1,Y_1),(X_2,Y_2)) \iff |X_1-X_2|+|Y_1-Y_2|=1. \]

This is Manhattan distance one.

12.2 Self-avoidance constraint

Two distinct amino acids cannot occupy the same lattice point:

\[ i\ne j\implies\varphi(i)\ne\varphi(j). \]

12.3 Energy

Non-consecutive amino acids can become spatial neighbours after folding. The energy is

\[ E(S,\varphi)= \sum_{1\le i\le n-2} \sum_{i+2\le j\le n} \operatorname{Pot}(s_i,s_j) \,[\operatorname{next}(\varphi(i),\varphi(j))]. \]

The potential used in the slide is

\[ \operatorname{Pot}(p,p) = \operatorname{Pot}(h,p) = \operatorname{Pot}(p,h)=0, \]

and

\[ \operatorname{Pot}(h,h)=-1. \]

The restriction \(j\ge i+2\) excludes adjacent amino acids already joined along the chain. Each non-consecutive hydrophobic contact contributes \(-1\), so minimising energy maximises favourable hydrophobic contacts.

The slide cites the result that deciding whether an HP sequence on the two-dimensional square lattice has a folding with energy below a threshold is NP-complete. This explains why complete exploration is difficult and connects the application back to the lecture’s optimisation and neighbourhood-search techniques.

13 Relationship between the two biological models

Application Observed input Hidden structure Objective or decision
haplotype inference genotypes over \(\{0,1,2\}\) explaining binary haplotypes minimise the number of distinct haplotypes, or decide whether \(k\) suffice
protein folding HP amino-acid sequence self-avoiding lattice conformation minimise contact energy

Both problems require selecting a discrete hidden explanation from a combinatorially large set. Both also contain strong constraints that eliminate most naïve assignments before optimisation.
