Bounds, Hyper-Arc, and Path Consistency

Lecture 4 - 9 October 2026

1 Local consistency revisited

The lecture continues the study of constraint propagation by comparing several levels of local consistency. Let

  • \(n\) be the number of variables;
  • \(c\) be the number of constraints;
  • \(d\) be the maximum domain size.

The previous lecture established the following coarse worst-case bounds:

\[ \text{Node consistency: }O(cd), \]

\[ \text{Arc consistency: }O(cnd^3). \]

For node consistency, at most \(c\) unary constraints are examined, and checking one unary constraint may require inspecting at most \(d\) domain values. Hence the total work is bounded by a constant multiple of \(cd\).

For arc consistency, revising one binary constraint may require comparing up to \(d^2\) pairs of values. Since at most \(nd\) values can be removed overall, revisions may be reactivated up to that order of magnitude, leading to the conservative bound

\[ O(nd\cdot cd^2)=O(cnd^3). \]

The notation \(O(\cdot)\) expresses asymptotic worst-case growth. It suppresses constant factors and lower-order terms and describes how the computational cost scales as the numbers of constraints, variables, and domain values increase.

All the propagation rules considered here are domain reduction rules: domains can shrink but never grow. The process is therefore monotone and eventually reaches a unique fixpoint, independent of the order in which valid reduction rules are applied.

2 Bounds consistency

Arc consistency checks every value in a domain. Bounds consistency is weaker: for numerical domains, it checks only the current minimum and maximum values.

Let \(c\) be a binary constraint on variables \(X\) and \(Y\), with domains \(D_X\) and \(D_Y\). The constraint is bounds consistent if:

  1. both \(\min(D_X)\) and \(\max(D_X)\) have supporting values in the interval from \(\min(D_Y)\) to \(\max(D_Y)\);
  2. both \(\min(D_Y)\) and \(\max(D_Y)\) have supporting values in the interval from \(\min(D_X)\) to \(\max(D_X)\).

Formally, the bounds of \(X\) require support:

\[ \exists b\in \min(D_Y)..\max(D_Y) \quad (\min(D_X),b)\in c, \]

\[ \exists b\in \min(D_Y)..\max(D_Y) \quad (\max(D_X),b)\in c. \]

Symmetrically, the bounds of \(Y\) require support:

\[ \exists a\in \min(D_X)..\max(D_X) \quad (a,\min(D_Y))\in c, \]

\[ \exists a\in \min(D_X)..\max(D_X) \quad (a,\max(D_Y))\in c. \]

A CSP is bounds consistent when each of its binary constraints is bounds consistent.

NoteWhat bounds consistency does not check

Bounds consistency verifies only the two extremes of each numerical domain. Unsupported internal values may remain. Consequently, bounds consistency normally removes fewer values than arc consistency.

2.1 Bounds consistent but not arc consistent

Consider

\[ \langle 2X=Y; X\in\{0,1,2\}, Y\in\{0,4\}\rangle. \]

The domain bounds are supported:

  • \(X=0\) is supported by \(Y=0\);
  • \(X=2\) is supported by \(Y=4\);
  • \(Y=0\) is supported by \(X=0\);
  • \(Y=4\) is supported by \(X=2\).

The CSP is therefore bounds consistent. However, the internal value

\[ X=1 \]

would require

\[ Y=2, \]

and \(2\notin D_Y\). Thus \(X=1\) has no actual support, so the CSP is not arc consistent. Applying arc-consistency filtering removes it:

\[ \langle 2X=Y; X\in\{0,2\}, Y\in\{0,4\}\rangle. \]

Consistency notion Scope of the check Main filtering effect
Node consistency One variable and unary constraints Removes values violating unary constraints
Arc consistency One binary constraint Every domain value must have support in the other domain
Bounds consistency Bounds of numerical domains Checks only current minima and maxima
Path consistency Three variables Every allowed pair must extend through a third variable
\(k\)-consistency \(k\) variables Every consistent assignment to \(k-1\) variables extends to the \(k\)th
Strong \(k\)-consistency All levels up to \(k\) The CSP is \(j\)-consistent for every \(j\leq k\)

3 Bounds-consistency reduction rules

For a binary constraint \(c\) on \(X\) and \(Y\), the bounds-consistency rules replace each domain by the interval between its smallest and largest supported values.

For \(X\), define the supported values

\[ S_X = \left\{ a\in D_X: \exists b\in\min(D_Y)..\max(D_Y) \text{ such that }(a,b)\in c \right\}. \]

Then

\[ D'_X = D_X\cap\bigl(\min(S_X)..\max(S_X)\bigr). \]

The rule is

\[ \text{(BC1)} \qquad \langle c;X\in D_X,Y\in D_Y\rangle \longrightarrow \langle c;X\in D'_X,Y\in D_Y\rangle. \]

Similarly, define

\[ S_Y = \left\{ b\in D_Y: \exists a\in\min(D_X)..\max(D_X) \text{ such that }(a,b)\in c \right\} \]

and

\[ D'_Y = D_Y\cap\bigl(\min(S_Y)..\max(S_Y)\bigr). \]

The symmetric rule is

\[ \text{(BC2)} \qquad \langle c;X\in D_X,Y\in D_Y\rangle \longrightarrow \langle c;X\in D_X,Y\in D'_Y\rangle. \]

Although these formulas are general, common arithmetic constraints admit much simpler specialised implementations.

3.1 Example: propagating \(X<Y\)

Start with

\[ \langle X<Y; X\in5..20, Y\in1..10\rangle. \]

The upper bound \(X=20\) has no support because no value in \(Y\) is greater than \(20\). For \(X<Y\) to hold, the largest supported value of \(X\) is

\[ \max(D_Y)-1=9. \]

Hence BC1 yields

\[ \langle X<Y; X\in5..9, Y\in1..10\rangle. \]

Now the lower bound \(Y=1\) has no support in \(X\in5..9\). The smallest supported value of \(Y\) is

\[ \min(D_X)+1=6. \]

BC2 therefore yields

\[ \langle X<Y; X\in5..9, Y\in6..10\rangle. \]

For constraints such as \(<\), \(=\), and \(\neq\), bounds can often be revised using a constant number of arithmetic operations rather than scanning every pair of values.

4 Complexity of bounds consistency

The lecture analyses a simple fixpoint algorithm that repeatedly applies bounds reductions to all constraints.

Assume a specialised bounds revision for one constraint takes almost constant time. A complete pass over all \(c\) constraints then costs

\[ O(c). \]

Comparing the domains before and after one pass costs at most

\[ O(n) \]

when the solver stores only numerical bounds for each of the \(n\) variables.

Across all variables, at most \(nd\) individual domain points can be removed. This provides a coarse upper bound of \(nd\) effective cycles. Multiplying the number of cycles by the cost per pass gives

\[ O(nd\cdot c) = \boxed{O(cnd)}. \]

Bounds consistency is therefore weaker but faster than full arc consistency. The appropriate choice depends on the CSP: stronger propagation may reduce more search, while cheaper propagation may be preferable when it is executed very frequently.

5 Queue-based propagation

Real constraint solvers do not normally rescan every constraint after every change. They maintain a propagation queue.

The scheduling policy introduced in the lecture is:

  1. at the root of the search tree, initialise the queue with all constraints;
  2. after a non-deterministic assignment to a variable \(X\), enqueue the constraints involving \(X\);
  3. remove one propagator from the queue and execute it;
  4. if the propagator changes the domain of a variable \(Y\), enqueue the propagators whose reasoning depends on \(D_Y\);
  5. stop when the queue becomes empty or a domain becomes empty.

This avoids reconsidering constraints that cannot be affected by the latest domain change.

5.1 Directional propagators for a binary constraint

A binary constraint \(c(X,Y)\) can be viewed as two directional revision operators:

\[ c(X,Y)_1: D_X \leftarrow \{a\in D_X:\exists b\in D_Y, (a,b)\in c\}, \]

and

\[ c(X,Y)_2: D_Y \leftarrow \{b\in D_Y:\exists a\in D_X, (a,b)\in c\}. \]

The first revises \(D_X\) using \(D_Y\); the second revises \(D_Y\) using \(D_X\).

If \(D_X\) changes, the revision that may newly become useful is \(c(X,Y)_2\), because supports in \(D_X\) for values of \(Y\) may have disappeared. Conversely, if \(D_Y\) changes, enqueue \(c(X,Y)_1\).

This directional view permits finer scheduling than treating each binary constraint as one indivisible operation. Further optimisations may use the constraint graph, variable ordering, or propagator-specific information.

6 Hyper-arc consistency

Arc consistency is defined for binary constraints. Hyper-arc consistency generalises the same support idea to a constraint involving \(m\geq2\) variables.

Let

\[ c\subseteq D_1\times\cdots\times D_m \]

be a constraint on

\[ X_1,\ldots,X_m. \]

The constraint is hyper-arc consistent if, for every variable \(X_i\) and every value \(a_i\in D_i\), there exist values for all the other variables such that the entire tuple satisfies \(c\):

\[ \forall i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}, \quad \forall a_i\in D_i, \]

\[ \exists a_1\in D_1,\ldots, \exists a_{i-1}\in D_{i-1}, \exists a_{i+1}\in D_{i+1},\ldots, \exists a_m\in D_m \]

such that

\[ (a_1,\ldots,a_i,\ldots,a_m)\in c. \]

A CSP is hyper-arc consistent when every constraint is hyper-arc consistent.

The property is also commonly called generalised arc consistency. The essential idea is unchanged: every current domain value must participate in at least one full satisfying tuple of the constraint.

6.1 Example: \(2X+3Y<Z\)

Consider

\[ \langle 2X+3Y<Z; X\in1..10, Y\in1..10, Z\in1..10\rangle. \]

The smallest possible left-hand side is obtained with \(X=1\) and \(Y=1\):

\[ 2\cdot1+3\cdot1=5. \]

Therefore, \(Z=1,2,3,4,5\) cannot be supported because the strict inequality requires \(Z>5\). Hence

\[ D_Z=6..10. \]

For \(Y=3\), even the smallest value \(X=1\) gives

\[ 2\cdot1+3\cdot3=11, \]

which cannot be strictly smaller than any \(Z\in1..10\). Thus \(Y=3\) and every larger value are unsupported, leaving

\[ D_Y=1..2. \]

Similarly, \(X=4\) is unsupported even with the smallest \(Y=1\):

\[ 2\cdot4+3\cdot1=11. \]

Therefore,

\[ D_X=1..3. \]

The hyper-arc-consistent CSP is

\[ \langle 2X+3Y<Z; X\in1..3, Y\in1..2, Z\in6..10\rangle. \]

7 Hyper-arc-consistency rules and complexity

For each variable position \(i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\), define the supported values

\[ D'_i = \left\{ a_i\in D_i: \text{there exist values in all other domains completing }a_i \text{ to a tuple in }c \right\}. \]

The rule \(\mathrm{HAC}_i\) replaces \(D_i\) by \(D'_i\) while leaving the other domains unchanged. Repeated application for every variable position and every constraint continues until a fixpoint or failure.

The deck states the conservative complexity

\[ \boxed{O(cnmd^{m+1})}, \]

where:

  • \(c\) is the number of constraints;
  • \(n\) is the number of variables;
  • \(m\) is the maximum constraint arity;
  • \(d\) is the maximum domain size.

The main factors can be understood as follows:

  1. for one \(m\)-ary constraint and one selected variable, up to \(d\) values may be tested;

  2. supporting one selected value may require exploring combinations of values for the remaining \(m-1\) variables, giving up to \(d^{m-1}\) combinations;

  3. revising every one of the \(m\) positions gives a cost on the order of \(md^m\) for one complete revision of one constraint;

  4. changes can trigger repeated revisions; a coarse bound uses up to \(nd\) value removals;

  5. multiplying by \(c\) constraints gives

    \[ c\cdot md^m\cdot nd = O(cnmd^{m+1}). \]

The exponential dependence on the arity \(m\) explains why specialised propagators and global-constraint algorithms are important for high-arity constraints.

8 Path consistency

Path consistency strengthens reasoning for binary CSPs by considering triples of variables.

8.1 Normalised and standard CSPs

A CSP is normalised when, for each unordered pair of variables \(X,Y\), there is at most one binary constraint on that pair. Denote it by

\[ C(X,Y) \]

when it exists.

If more than one binary constraint concerns the same pair, replace them by their intersection because constraints are sets of admitted tuples. For example, constraints \(C_1(X,Z)\) and \(C_2(X,Z)\) can be combined as

\[ C(X,Z)=C_1(X,Z)\cap C_2(X,Z). \]

A CSP is standard when every unordered pair of variables has exactly one binary constraint. When no explicit constraint exists between \(X\) and \(Y\), add the universal relation

\[ D_X\times D_Y, \]

which admits every pair and therefore does not exclude any assignment.

Every CSP can be rewritten into an equivalent standard CSP. During this rewriting, intersections may expose inconsistency and propagate domain reductions.

8.2 Example of standardisation

Suppose a CSP contains:

  • one binary constraint on \((X,Y)\);
  • two binary constraints on \((X,Z)\);
  • no binary constraint on \((Y,Z)\);
  • one ternary constraint on \((X,Y,Z)\).

To make the binary part standard:

  1. intersect the two relations concerning \((X,Z)\);
  2. add the universal relation \(D_Y\times D_Z\) for \((Y,Z)\);
  3. leave the ternary constraint unchanged.

Standardisation concerns the organisation of binary relations; it does not replace higher-arity constraints.

9 Composition and transpose of binary relations

For a binary relation \(R\), its transpose is

\[ R^T = \{(b,a):(a,b)\in R\}. \]

For binary relations \(R\) and \(S\), their composition is

\[ RS = \{(a,b):\exists c, (a,c)\in R \text{ and } (c,b)\in S\}. \]

The composition contains the endpoint pairs that can be connected through some intermediate value.

10 Definition of path consistency

A standard CSP is path consistent if, for every triple of variables \(X,Y,Z\),

\[ C(X,Z) \subseteq C(X,Y)C(Y,Z). \]

Equivalently, whenever

\[ (a,b)\in C(X,Z), \]

there must exist a value \(c\in D_Y\) such that

\[ (a,c)\in C(X,Y) \]

and

\[ (c,b)\in C(Y,Z). \]

Thus, every pair allowed directly between \(X\) and \(Z\) must be extendible through the third variable \(Y\).

The analogous requirements hold for the other two choices of endpoints:

\[ C(X,Y) \subseteq C(X,Z)C(Z,Y), \]

\[ C(Y,Z) \subseteq C(Y,X)C(X,Z). \]

11 Path-consistency example

Consider

\[ X\neq Y, \qquad Y\neq Z, \qquad X\neq Z, \]

with

\[ X,Y,Z\in\{0,1\}. \]

Each binary relation is

\[ C(X,Y)=C(Y,Z)=C(X,Z) = \{(0,1),(1,0)\}. \]

To compose \(C(X,Y)\) and \(C(Y,Z)\):

  • starting with \(X=0\), the first disequality forces \(Y=1\), and the second forces \(Z=0\);
  • starting with \(X=1\), the first forces \(Y=0\), and the second forces \(Z=1\).

Therefore,

\[ C(X,Y)C(Y,Z) = \{(0,0),(1,1)\}. \]

But

\[ C(X,Z) = \{(0,1),(1,0)\} \]

is not a subset of the composition. The CSP is not path consistent. This stronger check detects the contradiction that arc consistency alone did not detect.

12 Path-consistency proof rules

For a triple \(X,Y,Z\), path-consistency rules intersect each binary relation with the pairs that can be supported through the third variable.

The relation on \((X,Z)\) is revised as

\[ C'(X,Z) = C(X,Z) \cap \bigl(C(X,Y)C(Y,Z)\bigr). \]

The relation on \((X,Y)\) is revised as

\[ C'(X,Y) = C(X,Y) \cap \bigl(C(X,Z)C(Y,Z)^T\bigr). \]

The relation on \((Y,Z)\) is revised as

\[ C'(Y,Z) = C(Y,Z) \cap \bigl(C(X,Y)^T C(X,Z)\bigr). \]

The three corresponding rules are repeatedly applied for every triple until no binary relation changes or one becomes empty.

13 Complexity of path consistency

The deck provides a conservative polynomial bound. Using \(n\) variables and maximum domain size \(d\):

  • there are on the order of \(n(n-1)(n-2)=O(n^3)\) ordered variable triples;
  • checking or revising relations through a third variable can require nested scans over domain values, yielding a factor bounded by a power of \(d\);
  • binary relations themselves contain at most \(d^2\) pairs, and pair removals may trigger repeated cycles.

The resulting coarse bound stated in the lecture is

\[ \boxed{O(n^4d^5)}. \]

The important conclusion is comparative rather than the exact constant: path consistency reasons more strongly than arc consistency, but its substantially higher cost limits when it is practical.

14 Beyond path consistency

The final slide mentions \(k\)-consistency as the generalisation of the preceding notions. Informally, a CSP is \(k\)-consistent when every consistent assignment to \(k-1\) variables can be extended to any \(k\)th variable.

The deck explicitly postpones this topic, so no further development is included in this chapter.
