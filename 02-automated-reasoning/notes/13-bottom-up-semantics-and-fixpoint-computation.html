Bottom-Up Semantics and Fixpoint Computation

Lecture 13 - 18 November 2026

1 From logical consequences to bottom-up computation

For a definite-clause program \(P\), let \(B_P\) denote its Herbrand base and \(M_P\) its minimum Herbrand model. The preceding lecture established the central equivalence

\[ P \models A \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\in M_P \]

for every ground atom \(A\in B_P\).

The test \(A\in M_P\) can be approached top-down by SLD resolution. A successful SLD derivation not only establishes that \(A\) is a logical consequence of \(P\), but also explains which program clauses justify the conclusion. This explanatory derivation is relevant to explainable artificial intelligence: the proof structure records why the answer follows.

The present lecture develops the complementary bottom-up method. Instead of starting from a query, bottom-up evaluation starts from known facts and repeatedly derives every immediate consequence until a fixpoint is reached.

2 Grounding a definite program

Let \(H_P\) be the Herbrand universe of a program \(P\). The grounding of a rule is the set of all instances obtained by replacing every variable with every possible ground term from \(H_P\). The grounding of the complete program is denoted by

\[ \operatorname{ground}(P). \]

It may be finite or infinite, depending on the Herbrand universe.

2.1 Finite grounding

Suppose

\[ H_P=\{a,b\} \]

and the program contains the rule

q(X) :- r(X,Y), p(Y).

Every pair of substitutions for \(X\) and \(Y\) produces one ground instance:

q(a) :- r(a,a), p(a).
q(a) :- r(a,b), p(b).
q(b) :- r(b,a), p(a).
q(b) :- r(b,b), p(b).

The grounding contains four rules because each of the two variables has two possible ground values.

2.2 Infinite grounding

If

\[ H_P=\{0,s(0),s(s(0)),\ldots\}, \]

then the rule

num(s(X)) :- num(X).

has infinitely many ground instances:

num(s(0))       :- num(0).
num(s(s(0)))    :- num(s(0)).
num(s(s(s(0)))) :- num(s(s(0))).
...

The syntactic program is finite, but its grounding can be infinite because function symbols generate infinitely many ground terms.

3 The immediate-consequence operator

Let \(P\) be a definite-clause program and let \(I\subseteq B_P\) be a Herbrand interpretation. The immediate-consequence operator \(T_P\) returns all rule heads whose bodies are already true in \(I\):

\[ T_P(I) = \left\{ a \ \middle|\ \begin{array}{l} a\leftarrow b_1,\ldots,b_n\in\operatorname{ground}(P),\\ \{b_1,\ldots,b_n\}\subseteq I \end{array} \right\}. \]

For a fact, \(n=0\). Its body is empty and is therefore satisfied by every interpretation, including \(\varnothing\). Consequently, all facts of \(P\) belong to \(T_P(\varnothing)\).

The operator is called immediate because it applies the program rules once. Consequences that require several rule applications appear through repeated iterations of \(T_P\).

3.1 A finite bottom-up computation

Consider

r(a).
r(b).
p(a).
q(X) :- r(X), p(X).

Starting from the empty interpretation:

\[ T_P(\varnothing) = \{r(a),r(b),p(a)\}. \]

The ground instance

q(a) :- r(a), p(a).

now has its complete body in the interpretation, so the next application also derives \(q(a)\):

\[ T_P\bigl(\{r(a),r(b),p(a)\}\bigr) = \{q(a),r(a),r(b),p(a)\}. \]

A further application produces no change:

\[ T_P\bigl(\{q(a),r(a),r(b),p(a)\}\bigr) = \{q(a),r(a),r(b),p(a)\}. \]

The computation has reached a fixpoint.

Iteration Interpretation
\(0\) \(\varnothing\)
\(1\) \(\{r(a),r(b),p(a)\}\)
\(2\) \(\{q(a),r(a),r(b),p(a)\}\)
\(3\) unchanged, hence a fixpoint

4 Top-down and bottom-up behaviour

The two strategies compute the same declarative semantics, but their operational behaviour can differ substantially.

4.1 A looping top-down derivation

Consider

p(a).
q(X) :- q(X).

A top-down attempt to prove q(a) may repeatedly select the recursive rule:

\[ \leftarrow q(a) \Longrightarrow \leftarrow q(a) \Longrightarrow \leftarrow q(a) \Longrightarrow\cdots \]

At each step a fresh variable is introduced and unified with \(a\), but the selected goal remains q(a). SLD resolution may therefore loop.

Bottom-up evaluation terminates immediately:

\[ T_P(\varnothing)=\{p(a)\}, \]

and

\[ T_P(\{p(a)\})=\{p(a)\}. \]

The atom \(q(a)\) is never derived. The fixpoint is simply \(\{p(a)\}\).

4.2 Finite query, infinite bottom-up construction

Now consider

nat(0).
nat(s(X)) :- nat(X).

A top-down derivation for the specific query

?- nat(s(s(s(0)))).

is finite. Each resolution step removes one outer occurrence of \(s\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \leftarrow \operatorname{nat}(s(s(s(0)))) &\Longrightarrow \leftarrow \operatorname{nat}(s(s(0)))\\ &\Longrightarrow \leftarrow \operatorname{nat}(s(0))\\ &\Longrightarrow \leftarrow \operatorname{nat}(0)\\ &\Longrightarrow \square. \end{aligned} \]

The bottom-up construction instead enumerates the complete infinite model:

\[ \begin{aligned} T_P(\varnothing) &=\{\operatorname{nat}(0)\},\\ T_P^2(\varnothing) &=\{\operatorname{nat}(0),\operatorname{nat}(s(0))\},\\ T_P^3(\varnothing) &=\{\operatorname{nat}(0),\operatorname{nat}(s(0)), \operatorname{nat}(s(s(0)))\},\\ &\hspace{1em}\vdots \end{aligned} \]

No finite iteration contains every natural-number atom. The limit is

\[ \{\operatorname{nat}(0),\operatorname{nat}(s(0)), \operatorname{nat}(s(s(0))),\ldots\}. \]

Thus, in practice, naive bottom-up execution may continue forever even though the semantic limit is well defined.

Program and request Top-down behaviour Bottom-up behaviour
q(X) :- q(X) with query q(a) May loop on the recursive goal Immediately reaches \(\{p(a)\}\)
Naturals with one ground query Finite derivation for a fixed numeral Enumerates an infinite ascending chain

5 Models characterized by the immediate-consequence operator

Interpretations are ordered by inclusion in the complete lattice

\[ \left\langle\mathcal P(B_P),\subseteq\right\rangle. \]

The operator \(T_P\) provides a direct characterization of Herbrand models.

Theorem: Model characterization by \(T_P\)

For every Herbrand interpretation \(I\subseteq B_P\),

\[ I\text{ is a model of }P \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T_P(I)\subseteq I. \]

Every fixpoint \(T_P(I)=I\) is therefore a model of \(P\).

5.1 Proof

5.1.1 If \(T_P(I)\subseteq I\), then \(I\) is a model

Suppose

\[ T_P(I)\subseteq I. \]

Take any ground rule

\[ a\leftarrow b_1,\ldots,b_n \]

in \(\operatorname{ground}(P)\). If the body is not contained in \(I\), the implication is satisfied because its antecedent is false. If

\[ \{b_1,\ldots,b_n\}\subseteq I, \]

then \(a\in T_P(I)\). By the hypothesis \(T_P(I)\subseteq I\), it follows that \(a\in I\). Hence the rule is satisfied. Since this holds for every ground rule, \(I\) is a model of \(P\).

5.1.2 If \(I\) is a model, then \(T_P(I)\subseteq I\)

We prove the contrapositive. Suppose

\[ T_P(I)\setminus I\ne\varnothing. \]

Choose \(a\in T_P(I)\setminus I\). By the definition of \(T_P\), some ground rule

\[ a\leftarrow b_1,\ldots,b_n \]

has its body contained in \(I\), while its head \(a\) is absent from \(I\). That rule is false in \(I\), so \(I\) is not a model.

6 Monotonicity

Theorem: Monotonicity of \(T_P\)

For all Herbrand interpretations \(I,J\subseteq B_P\),

\[ I\subseteq J \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_P(I)\subseteq T_P(J). \]

6.1 Proof

Take any \(a\in T_P(I)\). Some ground rule

\[ a\leftarrow b_1,\ldots,b_n \]

has

\[ \{b_1,\ldots,b_n\}\subseteq I. \]

Since \(I\subseteq J\), the same body is contained in \(J\). Therefore \(a\in T_P(J)\).

Monotonicity ensures that iteration from the bottom produces an ascending chain:

\[ \varnothing \subseteq T_P(\varnothing) \subseteq T_P^2(\varnothing) \subseteq T_P^3(\varnothing) \subseteq\cdots \]

At any finite stage there are two possibilities:

  • if \(T_P^{n+1}(\varnothing)=T_P^n(\varnothing)\), a fixpoint has been reached;
  • if the inclusion is strict, the current interpretation is not yet a model because \(T_P(I)\nsubseteq I\).

7 Upward continuity

Monotonicity alone guarantees preservation of order. The following theorem establishes the stronger property used by the fixpoint construction.

Theorem: Upward continuity of \(T_P\)

For every ascending chain

\[ I_0\subseteq I_1\subseteq I_2\subseteq\cdots, \]

we have

\[ T_P\left(\bigcup_{i\ge 0} I_i\right) = \bigcup_{i\ge 0}T_P(I_i). \]

7.1 Proof

7.1.1 Left-to-right inclusion

Suppose

\[ a\in T_P\left(\bigcup_{i\ge0}I_i\right). \]

Then there is a ground rule

\[ a\leftarrow b_1,\ldots,b_n \]

whose body atoms all belong to the union. For each \(b_j\), choose an index \(k_j\) such that \(b_j\in I_{k_j}\). Because the body is finite, the maximum

\[ k=\max\{k_1,\ldots,k_n\} \]

exists. Since the chain is ascending, every body atom belongs to \(I_k\). Hence

\[ a\in T_P(I_k) \subseteq \bigcup_{i\ge0}T_P(I_i). \]

The finiteness of rule bodies is crucial here: finitely many witnessing stages can be replaced by one largest stage.

7.1.2 Right-to-left inclusion

Suppose

\[ a\in\bigcup_{i\ge0}T_P(I_i). \]

Then \(a\in T_P(I_k)\) for some \(k\). A ground rule derives \(a\) from body atoms contained in \(I_k\). Since

\[ I_k\subseteq\bigcup_{i\ge0}I_i, \]

the same rule derives \(a\) from the union. Thus

\[ a\in T_P\left(\bigcup_{i\ge0}I_i\right). \]

8 Computing the least fixpoint

Theorem: Bottom-up characterization of the minimum Herbrand model

For every definite-clause program \(P\),

\[ T_P\uparrow\omega = \operatorname{lfp}(T_P) = M_P. \]

Thus the bottom-up construction yields exactly the ground logical consequences of \(P\).

8.1 Proof

Define the upward iterates of \(T_P\) by

\[ T_P\uparrow 0=\varnothing, \]

\[ T_P\uparrow(n+1)=T_P(T_P\uparrow n), \]

and

\[ T_P\uparrow\omega = \bigcup_{n\ge0}T_P\uparrow n. \]

Upward continuity shows that the limit is a fixpoint:

\[ \begin{aligned} T_P(T_P\uparrow\omega) &=T_P\left(\bigcup_{n\ge0}T_P\uparrow n\right)\\ &=\bigcup_{n\ge0}T_P(T_P\uparrow n)\\ &=\bigcup_{n\ge0}T_P\uparrow(n+1)\\ &=T_P\uparrow\omega. \end{aligned} \]

Moreover, every model \(I\) contains every stage of this construction. The proof is by induction:

  • \(T_P\uparrow0=\varnothing\subseteq I\);
  • if \(T_P\uparrow n\subseteq I\), monotonicity gives \[ T_P\uparrow(n+1)=T_P(T_P\uparrow n)\subseteq T_P(I), \] and because \(I\) is a model, \(T_P(I)\subseteq I\).

Therefore the union of all stages is contained in every model.

9 Greatest fixpoints and downward iteration

The lecture also introduces the dual construction that starts from the complete Herbrand base:

\[ T_P\downarrow0=B_P, \]

\[ T_P\downarrow(n+1)=T_P(T_P\downarrow n), \]

and

\[ T_P\downarrow\omega = \bigcap_{n\ge0}T_P\downarrow n. \]

This descending iteration searches for the greatest fixpoint of \(T_P\).

Theorem: Knaster–Tarski and Kleene fixpoint consequences

Since \(T_P\) is monotone on the complete lattice

\[ \left\langle\mathcal P(B_P),\subseteq\right\rangle, \]

the Knaster–Tarski theorem guarantees a least and a greatest fixpoint:

\[ \operatorname{lfp}(T_P) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \operatorname{gfp}(T_P). \]

Since \(T_P\) is upward continuous, Kleene’s fixpoint theorem yields

\[ \operatorname{lfp}(T_P)=T_P\uparrow\omega. \]

The dual equality need not hold at \(\omega\). The operator is not necessarily downward continuous, so reaching the greatest fixpoint may require transfinite iteration beyond \(\omega\).

9.1 Example: why descending iteration may require more than \(\omega\)

Consider

r(0) :- r(0).
p(0) :- q(X).
q(s(X)) :- q(X).

The upward construction derives nothing:

\[ T_P\uparrow\omega=\varnothing. \]

For the descending construction, \(T_P\downarrow0=B_P\) contains every ground atom over the predicates \(r\), \(p\) and \(q\). The first stages are:

\[ \begin{aligned} T_P\downarrow1 &=\{r(0),p(0),q(s(0)),q(s(s(0))),\ldots\},\\ T_P\downarrow2 &=\{r(0),p(0),q(s(s(0))),q(s(s(s(0)))),\ldots\},\\ T_P\downarrow3 &=\{r(0),p(0),q(s(s(s(0)))),\ldots\}. \end{aligned} \]

Each finite stage removes one more \(q\)-atom from the beginning of the chain. Their intersection is

\[ T_P\downarrow\omega=\{r(0),p(0)\}. \]

This set is not a fixpoint:

\[ T_P(\{r(0),p(0)\})=\{r(0)\}. \]

Applying \(T_P\) once more produces \(\{r(0)\}\), which is the greatest fixpoint. Hence the construction needs a stage after \(\omega\).

Stage Remaining atoms, schematically
\(0\) all atoms in \(B_P\)
\(1\) \(r(0)\), \(p(0)\), and \(q\)-atoms from \(q(s(0))\) onward
\(2\) \(r(0)\), \(p(0)\), and \(q\)-atoms from \(q(s(s(0)))\) onward
\(\omega\) \(\{r(0),p(0)\}\), not a fixpoint
\(\omega+1\) \(\{r(0)\}\), the greatest fixpoint

The asymmetry between the least and greatest fixpoints is therefore structural: upward continuity guarantees that the least fixpoint is reached at \(\omega\), while lack of downward continuity allows the greatest-fixpoint construction to continue beyond \(\omega\).

10 Practical interpretation

For definite-clause logic programming, the declarative semantics used in the course is the minimum Herbrand model \(M_P\). It is the set of ground logical consequences and can be approached in two complementary ways:

  • top-down, by SLD resolution starting from a query;
  • bottom-up, by computing \(T_P\uparrow\omega\).

Top-down derivations can supply compact explanations for individual consequences, but may loop because of clause selection and recursion. Bottom-up evaluation is independent of a particular query and computes consequences systematically, but may not terminate operationally when the minimum model is infinite.

When the program has no function symbols, the Herbrand base is finite. In that case the ascending chain must stabilize after finitely many additions, and bottom-up evaluation reaches the minimum model in finite time.

The greatest fixpoint is relevant to coinductive interpretations, but the lecture only introduces this direction and its asymmetry with the least-fixpoint semantics.

11 Exercise direction

The lecture concludes by asking for short definite-clause programs whose logical consequences are computed in either of two ways:

  1. top-down through SLD resolution;
  2. bottom-up through repeated application of \(T_P\).

For a finite example, a complete solution should identify the ground instances used, list successive interpretations, detect the first repeated interpretation, and compare the resulting fixpoint with the answers obtained from top-down derivations.
